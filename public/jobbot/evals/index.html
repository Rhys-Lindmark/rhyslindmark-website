What changed in the tuned ranker

  1. Rank for a first interview. The current score adds fixed points. The tuned version multiplies the chance the candidate applies by the chance they get an interview after applying.
  2. Keep what you want and what you’ve done separate. Role and industry alignment come from the search description. Required-skill overlap and experience relative to the role’s seniority come from the résumé. The tuned ranker learns how to weigh each signal.
  3. Give dealbreakers their own inputs. The model sees partial work-mode matches, strict location or work-mode conflicts, pay below and above target, and whether pay was disclosed.
  4. Factor in who you know. A connection at the employer can move a job in the ranking beyond breaking ties. The experiment also used simulated employer competition, which would need a validated signal for live use.
  5. Learn from a wider set of jobs. After scoring each batch, the ranker learns from simulated applications and interviews on shortlist picks plus randomly selected jobs and roles related to the candidate’s search.

The benchmark measures these changes together. The draft uses the current scoring formula.

Method

The benchmark computes fit from synthetic résumés and preferences. Both rankers see the same job catalog in every round.

“Would pursue” is the simulated equivalent of a positive swipe. Expected interviews combine how likely a candidate is to apply with how likely the employer is to interview them if they apply. Modeled chances of a first interview assume independent outcomes within a shortlist.

Real evaluation starts with an optional “Would pursue” or “Pass” after the shortlist. Interview outcomes can be checked later among jobs people applied to, with missing outcomes tracked separately. Job offers are a longer-term outcome.
