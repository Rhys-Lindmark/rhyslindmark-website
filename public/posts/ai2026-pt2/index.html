1. Operating Margins of OAI & Ant

OpenAI and Anthropic are massive. They have over $100 billion of combined revenue.

They’re the fastest-growing companies we’ve ever seen.

There is a massive difference vs. Nvidia though — OAI & Ant have terrible margins.

OpenAI and Ant have ~40% gross margins.

This means they spend $60 on COGS (inference) to make $100 of revenue.

But OpenAI has -122% operating margin, and Anthropic's is just 5%.

This means that for every $100 of revenue, OpenAI spends $160 on opex (training R&D).

2. Long-horizon RL is the current frontier

Intelligence is the result of three inputs: compute, data, and algorithms. All of these are beginning to change. Let’s look at the progression.

The first LLMs used the most easily accessible data, the human internet slop. I love Karpathy’s framing here: early models compressed the 100 TB internet into 100 GB of parameters. Learning is compression.

After pretraining, LLMs are a lossy JPG of the internet; a document creator doing next-token prediction. After some chat RLHF, it nudges them to become good at chatting. And voilà, ChatGPT.

It took work to ingest the internet, but models were still dumb. Or at least their intelligence was spiky:

MMLU was the first big benchmark. It’s a hard test. Specific domain experts only get 90% in their target field. 175B param GPT-3 got 40%. 1.8T param MoE GPT-4 got 80%. It’s now been completely saturated.

GPQA (PhD-level questions) quickly became completely saturated too. Humanity’s Last Exam is the current frontier.

To beat all of these benchmarks, we needed a new training paradigm, Chain of Thought. Thinking step by step. I love Bob McGrew’s framing here:

“GPT-3, GPT-4, and GPT-5 each used 100x more compute for training.

o1 is packing 10,000x more compute into an answer.”

Fundamentally, o1 didn’t need new data from humans. No new internet-sized reasoning library. Instead, o1 needed PhD’s (from Mercor et al) to create difficult tasks and judging criteria. It needed verifiable rewards in domains like math and coding (RLVR).

Then o1 sculpts its own reasoning library through millions of reasoning trajectories. And boom, the model learns to think better thoughts. RL is bitter lesson pilled.

But we don’t just want the AIs to reason. It’s not enough to solve math problems.

Read Levent Alpöge’s post

We want AIs to do useful work. To be agents. To add to GDP.

Mostly this means being able to do lengthy tasks. Not just chat. Not just reason. But creatively pursue a goal.

This is the cutting edge of data. Agentic trajectories, not just reasoning trajectories. More than anything, agentic trajectories need an agentic harness. They need a while loop:

while (task != complete):
    use tools to complete task

With MCP, grep (read), and str_replace_editor (write), LLMs can start to write arbitrary code.

Give them a gym of tens of thousands of Mac Minis, and they can use any arbitrary computer application.

These agentic RL trajectories have shifted the data frontier from learning the human corpus to learning model trajectories.

Which has shifted the compute spend of training large models:

I love how Jie Tang of Z.ai talks about it in his post "Thoughts About Scaling Law."

Read Jie Tang’s post

He shows how we’ve moved from scaling pretraining compute to RL compute:

Total parameters appear to matter up to a threshold — enough to hold the world — after which additional capability comes from scaling elsewhere: effective depth per forward pass, and above all post-training.

GLM-5.3 is our controlled experiment on that claim. Same base, same architecture, same total and activated parameters as GLM-5.2.

One month of scaling long-horizon environments and RL. The gains are not marginal. Well, scaling has more than one dial. We turned the post-training one this time because it had the most slack left in it — not because the others are finished.

You can think of RL as another dimension for scaling laws.

Coming back to the three legs of the intelligence stool: compute, data, and algorithms.

Compute trains models on long-horizon RL tasks.
Data creates synthetic gyms for RL rollouts.
Algorithms emerge in this new RL paradigm.

In this case, it’s MOPD, Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation.
In MOPD, generalist models learn from trained RL experts.

These MOPD-trained agentic RL models are increasingly used to train themselves.

3. RL & the $10T labor market

Chatbots grew from 3,000 to 7.7M human full-time equivalents.

Coders grew from 1,000 to 30.5M—though that endpoint exceeds a plausible global developer-hour ceiling.

Researchers grew from 8,000 to 18.7M. Coders passed them around November 2025.

Autonomous driving grew from 189 to 13,000.

Humanoid robots grew from 29 to 7,000. Physical work remains far below digital work.

At $2.50 per million tokens, AI cognitive task-hours reach 57M full-time equivalents—165% of the top 34 white-collar occupations. These are task-hours, not jobs.

If the chips grew but models stayed at November 2022 quality, estimated work would reach 834,000 human-years per year.

Actual AI work grew from an estimated 13,000 to 57M human-years per year.

The shaded gap is about 56M human-years, or 68× the chips-only result. The ratio depends on the extrapolated starting value.

The marginal cost of coding has dropped precipitously. (I have 5+ agents coding random ideas I have, spending over a billion tokens a day.)

Cheap coding (and other cognitive labor) is a very useful service. OpenAI and Anthropic make over ~$50B/yr in revenue providing it. Even though their margins are small or negative, Chinese open-weight models are still trying to commoditize the model layer. They’re beginning to succeed.

For a while now, Chinese models have lagged behind US models by 7 months:

But the models generally have become wicked smaht. So being frontier doesn’t matter quite as much. US firms have shifted to Chinese models for their tokens:

Of course, OpenRouter isn’t all tokens. It’s likely less than 10%. They’re in the tens of trillions, while OAI and Ant are in the hundreds of trillions.

But this is impressive. Especially because DeepSeek, Kimi, and GLM were trained on only 1e25 FLOPs, while US foundation models use 1e26 or 1e27.
