Textiles and agriculture: workload models

These charts use the same idea as the software model: hold output fixed, split the original human workload into tasks, and show what happens when machines reduce the time needed for each task.

The time shares and reductions are illustrative assumptions, not measured historical statistics. The baseline is 100 units of labor for a fixed quantity of cotton cloth or grain. A stage’s remaining labor equals its baseline share multiplied by one minus its assumed reduction. Total speedup is 100 divided by the sum of the remaining labor.

The invention dates are historical milestones. The “after” case combines technologies and later improvements across centuries; it does not describe productivity in the invention year or claim that a single invention delivered the modeled reduction. It excludes maintenance, management, transport, sewing garments, crop growth time, and changes in output or demand.

Cotton textiles

TaskOriginal shareAssumed reductionLabor remaining
Picking cotton20%90%2%
Removing seeds10%95%0.5%
Spinning thread50%87.5%6.25%
Weaving cloth20%90%2%

Sum: 10.75% of the original labor remains, giving an illustrative 9.3× speedup. The chart rounds labels to one decimal. “Weaving clothes” refers here to making the cloth, not cutting and sewing garments. The spinning assumption uses an illustrative eightfold gain; spindle counts do not establish measured labor productivity.

  • Science Museum Group: Hargreaves’s spinning jenny — an early mechanization milestone, c. 1764–1765.
  • LSE economic history research on mechanized cotton production — Cartwright’s first power-loom patent was in 1785; later improvements and adoption followed.
  • Journal of Cotton Science: Development of the Cotton Gin — Whitney’s spike-tooth gin in 1794, followed by continuous-flow saw gins.
  • National Cotton Council: Contributions to Cotton Mechanization — the McCormick-Deering mechanical harvester was introduced in 1943, with broader adoption after World War II. Cotton picking was mechanized much later than spinning and weaving.
